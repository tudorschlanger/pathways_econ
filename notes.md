

# Lecture 1:

- I looked at the worksheet but I think it does not highlight the opportunity cost concept well enough, mainly because it bundles of 4-5 activities. It may confuse students. When a student finishes with "nap, study, hangout" what's the opportunity cost? Is it another bundle? But then what is the "value" of the next best bundle since we have to aggregate money and time into a common metric? 
- One way the opportunity cost idea could be better understood is if students go through example that build on each other and highlight a different angle at each step. Here are is an example of such sequence of questions : 
    1. The first slide should be the burger vs pizza slide, simple binary choice with $10 budget. 
    2. [Groups] Then have a slide with 3 alternatives, burger, pizza, cake -- emphasize value of next-best option
    3. Introduce time in the equation, no money. Maya can sleep in, go for a run, or finish her essay. She goes for the run; sleeping in was her second choice. The opportunity cost of the run is sleeping in.
    4. [Groups] "Free" things aren't free. A friend invites Maya to a free outdoor concert — three hours, no admission. She could instead study for Monday's test or work her shift at the cafe ($12/hr × 3 hrs = $36). The "free" concert costs her either better grades or $36. A great place to point out that "free" is one of the most misleading words in everyday life.
    5. Real decisions combine direct cost and opportunity cost. Maya goes to a movie. Ticket: $14. Snacks: $9. Three hours of her time, during which she could have earned $36. The true cost of the movie is roughly $59, not $23.
    6. [Groups] The big one — college vs. working full-time. After high school, Maya considers college versus starting full-time work. Tuition: ~$20,000/year. Foregone wages from a full-time job: ~$35,000/year. True cost: ~$55,000/year.
